Why Penske was losing new customers

47 interviews across five regions showed customers weren’t leaving over the wait for trucks — they were leaving at the rent-to-lease handoff, a maze the company itself couldn’t see.

Problem New customers were slipping away, and marketing blamed frustration over a two-year delivery wait.
What I owned Project Lead: research across 5 regions, synthesis, client relations, and the pivot to service design.
Team 2 designers and 2 researchers, via Zenda Consulting
Timeline About 5 months in 2022, extended long-term
Outcome The rent-to-lease billing transition named as the main driver of losses; 7 recommendations adopted.
Starting point

Penske assumed customers were leaving over a two-year wait

As pandemic-driven truck delivery delays mounted, Penske saw more new customers slip away. The marketing team assumed frustration over the two-year wait was to blame, and the brief was a 5-month messaging redesign.

But the pandemic had quietly turned Penske’s service from simple leasing into a complex rent-then-lease model, while the company’s structure stayed organized around pre-pandemic leasing. Corporate couldn’t see the friction, because field staff were patching it.

The research

47 interviews across five regions, from corporate offices to the field

We conducted 47 interviews across five regions — Reading, Rochester, Milwaukee, Sacramento, and Boston — with 9 corporate stakeholders, 30 field workers, and 8 customers navigating local mixed-fleet paths. Field research also included shadowing sales reps.

What we learned

Customers had made peace with the wait; the billing change was when they left

Customers weren’t frustrated by the two-year wait itself, but by Penske’s repeated failure to meet promised delivery dates — and above all by the rent-to-lease billing transition.

Most customers had made peace with the wait. The unexpected billing changes were the moment they left.

The rent-to-lease transition was the main driver of losses

We traced two customer journeys by service type, including the billing journey through both legacy product systems. Confusion set in at the handoff: invoices changed appearance, details grew harder to decipher, payment methods broke down, and new fuel receipt rules surfaced without warning. Unexplained additional costs were a red flag for small business owners, and decades-old loyal customers quit the service.

Every department had its own customer record

Shadowing showed how Lease Sales Representatives patched broken processes with personal spreadsheets, after-hours calls, and handwritten checklists tucked into notebooks. Those quiet fixes kept the real problems hidden from corporate leaders, while field staff absorbed the fallout and spent their day reconciling the gaps.

Penske’s customer onboarding was running on personal spreadsheets and handwritten checklists: invisible to corporate, fragile by design.

Smaller operators were an invisible segment

Penske’s onboarding ran a single rhythm across customers who needed very different ones. Smaller operators without purchasing staff were hit hardest by the misalignment — a vulnerable segment no one had been looking at.

Making it visible

Three artifacts put the customer’s maze in front of every department

The Rhythm of Work model

Every business runs on its own cadence, shaped by size, number of locations, financial calendar, product-release cycle, client needs, and seasonal events. Mapping Penske’s local, customer-led, and corporate-led actions against each customer cadence is what surfaced the smaller operators.

The Customer Start-up Visual

The client’s most valued diagnostic tool placed the customer at the center, making the maze of scattered contact points visible from their perspective for the first time. It united departments around the customer’s true journey as a shared starting point for redesign.

The operations map

A detailed operations model traced every process step in the client journey, the staff responsible, the data needed to give customers the right onboarding support, the common human errors at each stage, and the tools and daily workarounds staff relied on. I printed it at A1 size to show the institutional breakdown at each step where the customer experienced frustration.

Outcome

A 5-month messaging brief became a long-term service-design engagement

The findings set the strategic direction for service redesign at key high-value customer touchpoints.

Long-term engagement after the pivot, extended from a 5-month brief
7 recommendations adopted
2 named models became cross-departmental reference
1 previously invisible customer segment surfaced
  • The rent-to-lease billing transition was named as the primary driver of customer loss, with a standardization recommendation accepted by the billing organization.
  • The Customer Start-up Visual was adopted as Penske’s shared cross-departmental reference for the customer journey.
  • The mobile app product team rerouted onto a service-design-first roadmap after the engagement showed adoption was a service-design problem.
  • Boston’s local delivery-delay management system was identified as a best practice to scale across other districts.
  • The recommendations covered workflow management, master data strategy, billing standardization, tiered services by customer group, and a plan to expand field-level innovations.
The same method at Johnson & Johnson

Mapping how product-label content moved through J&J

J&J’s Global Labeling Operations team wanted to go Digital First, but no one had a clear view of how ecommerce product content moved from creation to the final product page. Gaps between teams were hidden, covered by individuals doing manual work.

Role Senior Design Strategist, via Zenda Consulting
Team 3 people: project lead, researcher, and me
Timeline 8 weeks, 2023

Interviews and collaborative mapping

I led in-depth interviews with directors, managers, and team leads. People were open about their frustrations; the problems were clear, but no one had brought them together in one place. We then mapped with stakeholders how content moved from creation to modification, storage, and digital execution, which made it clear where teams were disconnected.

I organized the operations into a Data Operations model: four areas around a central Digital Content Ecosystem — Upstream (gathering label data), Performance (understanding and learning), Delivery (label delivery), and Downstream (label curation). We weighted each gap by the manual work it required, the number of teams affected, and the extent of the impact, turning the model into a roadmap for leadership.

What surfaced

  • Post-performance data was under-used. Teams working on digital execution could see how products performed; teams making early content decisions often could not.
  • Ownership of the data lifecycle was scattered. Handoffs lacked a shared understanding, and new staff learned by following the most experienced people.
  • Disconnected systems forced manual workarounds. Content updates often went back two or three times before going live.
Teams creating product content could not see how consumers reacted to it. Teams who saw the consumer response could not influence what was created.

Outcome

The Data Operations model became a shared tool for leadership to spot communication gaps and opportunities across the content lifecycle. For the first time, directors and managers had a single view of how content moved through the organization, and the conversation shifted from documenting the process to deciding where and when to invest — groundwork for the Digital First strategy.
