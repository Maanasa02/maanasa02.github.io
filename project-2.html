Testing a field tool for San Francisco’s outreach workers

A rapid prototype that put cross-departmental client data in outreach workers’ hands — and showed the decisions were being made one level up.

Problem Eight street teams across four departments met the same clients with no shared view of their history or eligibility.
What I owned Design lead: use-case workshops across 4 departments, then design and build of the tool.
Team 4-person team (data scientist, PM, project lead) across 4 city departments
Timeline 2023 – present
Outcome Testing moved the primary user from field workers to managers, redirecting investment to the manager-facing view.
Starting point

Eight street teams, four departments, no shared view of the client

Eight street-response teams operated across four San Francisco departments under overlapping mandates. Each owned a slice of the same client’s journey, and none could see what the others were doing. A worker meeting someone on the street had no way to know who had worked with them before or what they were eligible for.

Co-design

Use-case workshops across all four departments defined what a field tool had to do

I conducted use-case workshops across the four departments to design and build the outreach worker tool. The field worker’s need, as we framed it:

“When I meet a client on the street, I want their history and resource eligibility right there at the point of contact, so I can act instead of starting from zero.”
The bet

Put cross-departmental client data in field workers’ hands at the point of contact

The hypothesis: if outreach workers could see integrated client data — history, resource eligibility, and prior contacts — at the moment they met someone, they could act faster.

The tool was built in days with Lovable, and its job was to test that hypothesis, not to ship a polished product. The use patterns would do the arguing.

Key decisions

Deliberately low fidelity, showing only what a worker needs on the street

  • Rapid prototype, built in days. Low fidelity on purpose: the point was to learn whether field workers could use the data, not to polish an app before knowing.
  • One lookup, one client view. A worker looks a client up by name and date of birth, then sees housing info, behavioral history, shelter and ER history, points of contact, and encounter history drawn from across departments.
What testing found

Field workers weren’t the ones making the call

Testing disproved the assumption. Field workers have little in-the-moment decision power: when they meet a client who needs cross-departmental resources, the real call happens a level up. So the primary user shifted from field workers to their managers.

Outreach / field worker

Needs to know who has worked with this client before, and what they’re eligible for.

  • Has little in-the-moment decision power — the real call happens a level up.
Manager / command lead

“When a frontline worker calls me about a client, I want to make the coordination and resourcing decision across departments, so the right resource actually gets reserved.”

  • This is where field decisions actually get made.
  • Needs to see the client across departments to prioritize and assign owners.
Outcome

The finding redirected investment to the manager-facing view

Because the decisions lived with managers, I invested higher fidelity in the manager-facing surface instead of polishing the field app. That view, Rapid Access to Cross-Departmental Client Data, brings each department’s slice of a client into one screen so a manager can make the coordination and resourcing call.

What I’d carry forward

Fidelity follows the decision

  • 01

    Match tool fidelity to where the decision actually happens. A fast, disposable prototype in the field was worth more than a polished app aimed at people who couldn’t act on it.

  • 02

    Build to test a hypothesis, not to ship. A prototype built in days let real use settle the question, and a disproved assumption pointed the investment somewhere better.
