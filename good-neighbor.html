Core skill · Taking a product from 0 to 1

Good Neighbor: from scattered chats to one reporting tool in under 3 months

San Francisco’s Good Neighbor Policy asks service providers to check the area around their sites and report what they find. I led design and research on the tool that gives them one place to do it — and to see what happens after they report.

Problem Each provider reported through its own systems and chat groups, with no clear record of what happens after a report.
What I owned All design and research, from vision to release-ready.
Team Me, 1 PM (who also tested in the field), 1 engineer
Timeline Under 3 months
Outcome Release-ready; launching before December 2026, still testing with 15–20 providers.
Starting point

Every provider had its own system, and no one knew what happened after a report

Under the Good Neighbor Policy, service providers check the area around their sites and report what they find. Before this tool, each provider ran its own systems and Google Chat groups. Some tried calling department heads directly.

There was no clear documentation of what happens after a report is made.

Aligning leadership

Making the case for a service, not a compliance tool

The stakeholders were the Mayor’s Chief of Homelessness and Supportive Housing, the DPH Public Health Director, the DPH Strategy Director, and the HSH communications and data leads. The big tension: was this a compliance tool or a service tool?

I pushed for giving providers a real benefit, so they would actually use it. A compliance-only tool would have failed. What moved leaders was research — stories and ideas from providers, and a clear account of what was at stake.

Research and testing

About 5 providers shaped the idea; 15–20 are shaping the build

I started with product-market-fit research with about 5 providers. Testing has since grown to 15–20 providers and is still going, with our PM joining me in the field during the build.

Three changes came straight from testing:

  • A restructured perimeter check.
  • Clearer copy throughout.
  • Ways to update a report and add more information to it.
Key decisions

Six decisions that keep the tool worth using

  1. 01

    A walk around the building becomes three named sides

    Front, Sides and Back are each recorded with a photo or a typed note. “Finish check” completes once all three are recorded.

  2. 02

    The AI sorts findings into the right route as you go

    Each finding goes to its route: a 311 request, an on-site action, or a non-emergency call. A duplicate of an open ticket merges right away into a short receipt — “Added as an update to #P1-0033” — instead of creating a new job.

  3. 03

    A person always files the 311

    Filing is an explicit “File 311 request” button. It never happens automatically.

  4. 04

    Cards say what was found and ask one question

    “Were you able to pick this up?” The app doesn’t tell clerks how to do their job. The only exceptions are phone numbers and safety warnings, like “Never pick one up yourself.”

  5. 05

    Three tabs, one mental model

    To do is what you can still act on. In progress is waiting on someone else — a filed 311, a call made — with statuses like “Call confirmed”, “City response overdue” and “Scheduled”. History is settled. A ticket is never in two lists.

  6. 06

    Several sites, one tap

    Managers with several sites switch between them in one tap. Single-site accounts never see the switcher.

Where it stands

Release-ready, and launching before December 2026

A team of three — me, a PM and an engineer — took Good Neighbor from vision to release-ready in under 3 months. It hasn’t been released yet; it launches before December 2026.

Until then, and after, we keep testing with providers.

What I’d carry forward

A tool people have to use still has to be worth using

  • 01

    Compliance alone doesn’t get a tool used. Making the case for what providers get out of it, with research behind it, is what aligned leadership.

  • 02

    Let the AI do the sorting, and keep people in charge of the actions that matter. A person always files the 311.
