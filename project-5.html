Core skill · Mapping and redesigning how teams work together

Redesigning how four city departments coordinate care

How I mapped San Francisco’s street response across four departments and turned it into a shared way of working — a common goal, a weekly Shared Priority List, and the data foundation underneath.

Problem Roughly 10% of the unhoused population used 50% of services, cycling through emergency response with no team owning the outcome.
What I owned Design lead: research, operations mapping across 4 departments, goal-setting workshops, the Shared Priority List.
Team 4-person team (data scientist, PM, project lead) across 4 city departments
Timeline 2023 – present
Outcome 2/3 of the 15 highest-acuity pilot clients moved into housing, shelter, treatment or case management within four months.
The problem

Eight street teams, four departments, and no one owning the outcome

Eight street-response teams worked across four city departments under overlapping mandates, with no shared view of the client. Roughly 10% of the unhoused population used 50% of all services, cycling through emergency response without moving toward stable care or housing.

Each team owned a slice of the same client’s journey. No one owned the outcome.

What I found

Clients stalled for the same four reasons — an accountability problem, not a data problem

I ran 30+ ride-alongs across DPH, Fire, DEM and HSH, plus 45+ interviews with field staff and leadership. The same pattern came up again and again.

Ride-alongs mostly ended with clients left on the street for one of four reasons: staff couldn’t diagnose the need; the need was diagnosed but the client wasn’t eligible; the client was eligible but the resource wasn’t available; or the resource was available but the client wouldn’t take it.

The team had been solving a data-integration problem. Six weeks in, I pushed to reframe it: the real work was redesigning accountability across departments.

What I mapped

Mapping four departments’ operations exposed every handoff and breakpoint

I mapped how a client moves across the four departments: every handoff, and every breakpoint where they fall out. I built the service blueprint on walls with city leaders and frontline staff, so leaders felt ownership of it.

What I designed

A shared goal, then a weekly list of who needs coordinated care most

I designed and facilitated six theory-of-change workshops with leaders from all four departments, each grounded in real client stories. They produced the first street-response goal DPH, Fire, DEM and HSH jointly held, with named ownership for handoffs no one had owned before.

That goal became a weekly ritual: the Shared Priority List of clients with the most cross-departmental encounters and the most complex needs. Five prompts — next steps, coordination, required resources, measuring success and case review — keep every review on the same ground. It stayed a service, not an app.

Underneath sits ASTRID, the shared cross-departmental dataset. I drafted the first version of the legal agreement that made sharing it lawful, the Homeless Multidisciplinary Data Trust.

Outcome

Coordinated care moved the highest-need clients — and became the city’s model

The pilot followed 15 of the highest-acuity clients for four months.

2/3 moved into housing, shelter, treatment, or case management within four months
0 deaths and overdoses in the pilot cohort
−50% hospital transports

ASTRID is now the operational backbone of Mayor Lurie’s Neighborhood Street Teams model.

  • Selected for a 3-year, $7M Bloomberg Philanthropies partnership.
What I’d carry forward

Ask who owns the outcome before deciding what to build

  • 01

    Reframing from data integration to accountability was the real unlock. The leverage came from asking who owned the outcome, not which datasets to merge.

  • 02

    Getting leaders to co-build the artifact on the wall created ownership. When department chiefs drew the blueprint themselves, they bought into using it.
